Sudoku Mathematics, Complexity & Combinatorics

Answering foundational questions on game theory, state spaces, algorithmic generation, and computational limits.

1. Is Sudoku a Solved Game?

In game theory and mathematics, whether a game is "solved" depends on the definition used:

• For standard 9×9 Sudoku: Yes, it is exhaustively analyzed and computationally solved. In 2005, Bertram Felgenhauer and Frazer Jarvis proved the exact number of valid filled $9 \times 9$ Sudoku grids is 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960. Taking into account all symmetries (rotations, reflections, permutations of rows/columns within bands, and digit relabeling), Ed Russell and Frazer Jarvis proved there are exactly 5,472,730,538 essentially different equivalence classes.

• The 17-Clue Minimum Proof: In January 2012, Gary McGuire, Bastian Tugemann, and Gilles Civario proved via an exhaustive 7.1 million CPU-hour supercomputer search that no 16-clue Sudoku can ever have a unique solution. Standard Sudoku requires a mathematical minimum of 17 clues.

• For generalized N×N Sudoku (n² × n²): Determining whether an arbitrary puzzle has a valid solution is NP-complete (proved by Takayuki Yato and Takahiro Seta, 2003). While computers solve $9 \times 9$ boards in under a millisecond using backtracking or Donald Knuth's Dancing Links (Algorithm X), scaling the grid to $100 \times 100$ quickly becomes intractable in the worst case.

2. Can We Generate All Combinations?

It depends on whether you mean completed grids or partial clue puzzles:

• All 6.67 × 10²¹ Raw Grids: Sequential output of all raw grids is practically impossible due to cosmic time scales. Even if a computer generated 1 billion grids per second without pausing, it would take 211,000 years to output all $6.67 \times 10^{21}$ grids. Storing them in raw uncompressed text would require roughly 540,000 exabytes of storage!

• All 5.47 Billion Symmetry Equivalence Classes: Yes! This has been done. The 5,472,730,538 fundamental classes have been completely catalogued and indexed by mathematicians into compressed databases of roughly 40 to 60 gigabytes.

• All Valid Puzzles (Starting Clue Layouts): The space of all partial configurations is roughly $3^{81} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{38}$. Even filtering only for those with a single unique solution leaves an estimated $10^{30}$ to $10^{35}$ valid puzzles. Storing all of them is physically impossible—which is why modern software generates them procedurally on demand!

3. Are Puzzles Generated On-The-Fly or Handcrafted?

Almost all modern Sudoku puzzles are generated 100% on the fly!

Here is the exact 4-stage algorithm used by modern game engines (and running directly in this app):

1. FULL GRID GENERATION: Start with an empty 9×9 board. Fill cells using randomized backtracking with constraint propagation (MRV heuristic). Takes: ~1 to 3 milliseconds. 2. SYMMETRIC HOLE DIGGING: Group cells into 180° rotationally symmetric pairs: (r, c) and (8-r, 8-c). Shuffle the pairs into random order. For each pair: Temporarily clear both cells. Check uniqueness: countSolutions(board, limit=2) === 1. If still strictly unique: keep cells cleared! If ambiguous (>1 solutions): rollback and restore numbers! 3. DIFFICULTY TARGETING: Continue digging until clue target is reached: Easy (35-40 clues) • Medium (29-34 clues) • Hard (25-28 clues) • Expert (21-24 clues). 4. LOGICAL DEDUCTION GRADING: Run a human-style solver (Singles → Pairs → Intersections → X-Wings → Wings). Grade the puzzle's difficulty based on the hardest technique required to solve it without guessing!

Why did people design them by hand? In the 1980s and 1990s, the Japanese publisher Nikoli (led by Maki Kaji) popularized handcrafted Sudokus. Nikoli's human designers treated puzzle creation as an art form, insisting that every puzzle have aesthetic 180° rotational symmetry, beautiful clue patterns, and elegant logical step transitions without trial-and-error guessing. Today, procedural computer algorithms can enforce those same aesthetic and logical constraints automatically in just a few milliseconds.
